Robot-as-Sensor Joint-Angle Estimation for Casualty Manipulation

A robot holding a casualty's limb corrects the camera's joint-angle estimate, fusing an RGB-D pose network with arm and hand proprioception in a grasp-aware error-state EKF.

Casualty manipulation perception

Can a robot that is holding a casualty's limb estimate the casualty's joint angles better than a camera alone? This project treats the robot itself as a sensor: a UR5 arm with a LEAP hand grasps a lying person's forearm or shank, and its own arm and hand encoders and wrist force are fused with an RGB-D pose network in a grasp-aware error-state EKF. Everything is built on an anatomical, physics-simulated human ("caveman") in Isaac Sim with a ROS 2 interface.

Story

During casualty manipulation the camera is at its weakest exactly when the estimate matters most: the person is lying, limbs are self-occluded or hidden by the robot, and the pose being produced is unusual. A pose network also tends to misread a held limb the same way for many seconds. The robot, however, knows precisely where its palm is and that it is holding the limb. The question is whether that grasp geometry can correct the camera's persistent error, using only signals a real robot has: arm and hand encoders, finger closure, and wrist force.

Problem formulation

A casualty lies on the floor in view of a static RGB-D camera. The robot grasps the distal forearm or shank, lifts or slides it, moves it, and releases it. At each camera frame $t$ the goal is the casualty's joint angles $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$, reported on shoulder and elbow flexion for the forearm (hip and knee for the shank), over the whole manipulation from grasp to release. The available signals are:

  • Vision: joint angles $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t$ and pelvis pose $\hat T_{P,t}$ from the pose network, and a body scale $\hat\kappa$ from depth.
  • Robot: the palm pose $T_{H,t}$ from arm and hand encoders, the finger gap $\Delta_t$, and the wrist force $\mathbf{F}_t$.

The filter state collects the pose, the grasp, the body scale, and the vision bias:

$$ \mathbf{x}=\left(\boldsymbol{\theta},\ T_P,\ G,\ \kappa,\ \mathbf{b}\right), $$

where $T_P$ is the pelvis pose, $G$ the grasp (the limb relative to the palm), $\kappa$ a uniform body scale, and $\mathbf{b}$ a slowly varying vision bias. Treating the network's error as persistent rather than white is the key modelling choice: the vision residual is

$$ \mathbf{r}_v=\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{b}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t , $$

and the bias decays with a time constant $\tau$ chosen on validation people. While the grasp gate $h_t$ is on, the robot contributes a grasp line $\boldsymbol{\ell}_t$: the limb axis predicted from the palm, $(\tilde{\mathbf{p}},\tilde{\mathbf{u}})$ from $T_{H,t}G^{-1}$, must agree with the axis $(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{u})$ of the estimated limb given by $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, $T_P$ and $\kappa$:

$$ \mathbf{r}_g= \begin{bmatrix} \left(I-\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\right)\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}-\mathbf{p}\right)\\ \tilde{\mathbf{u}}\times\mathbf{u} \end{bmatrix}. $$

Only the perpendicular offset and the direction are constrained; sliding along the limb and rolling about it are left free, because a compliant hand is a ring around the limb rather than a clamp. The predict step moves the limb with the measured palm displacement and decays the bias, and the update is iterated on the manifold:

$$ \mathbf{x}_t^{-}=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+},\ \delta T_{H,t}\right), \qquad \mathbf{x}_t^{+}=\mathbf{x}_t^{-}\boxplus K\mathbf{r}. $$

Method

Pivot model. During the lift the forearm tilts 14–23° inside the hand, always the same way. The grasp line therefore starts at the planned grasp $G_0$ at onset and is turned about the elbow as the palm rises from its pose at grasp $T_{H,t_0}$. This cuts the median axis-direction error from 17.5° to 5.4°.

Grasp gate. The grasp is a measurement only while the finger encoders show the hand closed on the limb (a hysteretic finger-gap band) and the wrist force before the lift stays below the UR5's 150 N protective stop. Only grasps the robot itself accepts are evaluated.

Pose network. The vision model reads segmented RGB and depth aligned to colour in a person-centred crop with eight frames of history, using a DINOv3-L backbone and a causal temporal transformer. It predicts 12 primary flexion angles plus a pelvis head (3.5 cm and 7.0° on test people).

Body scale. Head-to-heel length from the first depth frame, divided by the template's, gives $\hat\kappa$ to within 0.3% of the true length.

Simulated human and dataset

The caveman is built on NVIDIA's rigged people with 32 anatomical degrees of freedom, human ranges of motion (including a shoulder joint sinus), de Leva segment masses, soft-tissue collision hulls, and passive joint mechanics so that a relaxed or unconscious body settles realistically. A ROS 2 Jazzy interface exposes joint states, commands, and active, passive, or effort control.

The dataset contains 14,586 sequences (700,128 frames) of 36 people, split by person, from a simulated RealSense D435 with stereo noise, shadows, and dropout. Activities range from walking, falling, and crawling to 5,476 lying sequences, labelled with 32 joint angles at 30 and 120 Hz, keypoints, person masks, and camera poses.

Robot study

A UR5 with a full LEAP hand at its real gains and 550 mA current limit, with a flange force/torque sensor, runs at 600 Hz physics next to a static D435 model at 30 Hz. The robot grasps the distal forearm and lifts it about 8 cm, or slides the shank on its heel, moves the limb, and releases it. The grasp is planned with vision-level error. Every setting is chosen on validation people; the test is a fresh confirmation set of 72 episodes on 4 test people, generated after the method was fixed.

The robot removes 37% of the forearm vision error over the whole manipulation, and about 45% while it holds the arm. Every test person gains (+33 to +48%) on both arms, and the pivot model gets within 7 points of a line through the true limb axis. Prior trackers given the same inputs lose to vision: a rigid grasp ignores that the arm turns in a compliant hand, and held-point tracking accumulates the error of its unknown start. Only when given the casualty's true start posture, which cannot be measured on an unresponsive person, does Zhu et al. match the mean, with a wider spread (95th percentile 15.3° vs. 12.6°).

Without the bias state the gain vanishes: the vision error is persistent, and modelling it as a slowly varying bias is what lets the grasp correct it. The palm displacement carries the estimate when the camera cannot see the limb.

Single-image 3D pose models place the body 10–20 cm off and jitter frame to frame. Anchoring their pelvis with depth and giving them their own per-frame noise (both chosen on validation) lets the filter improve them too; with the robot, SAM 3D Body reaches 5.6° on the forearm.

Limitations and next steps

Shank. The leg is slid on its heel rather than lifted, because a straight leg needs more ankle force than the arm and hand can carry, so it moves little and the planned axis is never corrected by a lift; 13 of 24 shank grasps hit the protective stop. The shank gain is therefore small (+8.6% on 7 accepted grasps).
In-grip turning. While the arm is moved it also turns 3–6° inside the compliant grip. The grasp line is fixed in the palm after the hold starts, so the estimate gets the elbow's level right but not its faster swings. Modelling that turn, as the pivot model does for the lift, is the next step.
All results so far are in simulation. A hardware study with the lab's UR5, LEAP hand, and force/torque sensor, first on a mannequin and then on people, is planned.
