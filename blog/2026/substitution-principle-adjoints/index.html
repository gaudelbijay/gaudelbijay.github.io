§4 — Convergence: The Safety Net

Infinite sums are delicate. Rearranging terms can change the value — or make it diverge. Absolute convergence is the safety net: it licenses all regrouping, all rearranging. Every algebraic identity that works for scalars then carries over to matrices unchanged.

§1.1 · 5 — Exponential of a Jordan block

Question. For the n by n Jordan block J = lambda I + N, show that exp(J) has e^lambda/k! on the kth superdiagonal.

Solution. Write the Jordan block as J = lambda I + N, where N has 1 on the first superdiagonal and N^n = 0. The scalar part lambda I commutes with N, so the exponential separates into exp(lambda I) exp(N). This is the key substitution move: the power series sees lambda I and N as commuting inputs.

Now N^k has 1 on the kth superdiagonal and 0 elsewhere. Therefore the kth superdiagonal of exp(J) is exactly e^lambda/k!. The nilpotence is not a technicality: it is what turns the infinite exponential series into a finite polynomial, so the Jordan block is the cleanest place to see how the substitution principle becomes computable.

§1.1 · 6 — Exponential of the derivative operator

Question. Let E be the polynomials of degree at most n - 1 and D = d/dxi. Show that exp(tau D)f(xi) = f(xi + tau).

Solution. The operator D is nilpotent on this finite-dimensional polynomial space because differentiating n times kills every polynomial of degree at most n - 1. Thus exp(tau D) is a finite operator polynomial, not a mysterious infinite object.

The last expression is Taylor expansion of f at xi evaluated at xi + tau. So exp(tau D) is the translation operator. This is one of the best examples of the whole article: the same exponential recipe works after substituting a differential operator for a number, and its geometric effect is visible as shifting the input.

§1.2 · 1 — Nilpotent matrices and unipotent matrices

Question. Show that X -> exp X maps nilpotent matrices bijectively onto unipotent matrices, with inverse a -> log a.

Solution. If X is nilpotent, then X^m = 0 for some m. The exponential series terminates, so exp X is I plus a polynomial in X with zero constant term. That extra part is nilpotent because it is a polynomial in a nilpotent matrix with no constant term. Hence exp X is unipotent.

Conversely, if a is unipotent, then a = I + N with N nilpotent. The logarithm series also terminates:

The formal identities exp(log(1+z)) = 1+z and log(exp z) = z become finite polynomial identities after substituting nilpotent matrices. That proves both directions and the inverse relation. The depth here is that nilpotence removes all analytic danger; algebra alone carries the result.

§1.2 · 2 — Semisimple matrices and the exponential

Question. Show that exp maps semisimple matrices to semisimple matrices, that every invertible semisimple complex matrix is an exponential of a semisimple matrix, and explain the logarithm ambiguity.

Solution. A complex semisimple matrix is diagonalizable. If X = PDP^{-1}, then the exponential is computed entrywise on the diagonal because powers of D remain diagonal.

So exp X is diagonalizable in the same eigenbasis, hence semisimple. Conversely, if a = P diag(mu_1,...,mu_n) P^{-1} is invertible semisimple, choose any logarithm lambda_j of each nonzero eigenvalue mu_j and set X = P diag(lambda_1,...,lambda_n) P^{-1}. Then X is semisimple and exp X = a.

The ambiguity is exactly the scalar logarithm ambiguity: lambda_j may be replaced by lambda_j + 2 pi i k_j. If no two eigenvalues differ by a nonzero multiple of 2 pi i, then the exponential does not accidentally merge distinct eigenspaces. This explains why semisimple logarithms are not unique, but their nonuniqueness is controlled and spectral.

§1.2 · 3 — Jordan decomposition and the exponential

Question. Relate additive Jordan decomposition X = Y + Z to multiplicative Jordan decomposition of exp X.

Solution. In the additive Jordan decomposition, Y is semisimple, Z is nilpotent, and YZ = ZY. Because the two pieces commute, the exponential separates:

By the semisimple result, e^Y is semisimple. By the nilpotent-unipotent result, e^Z is unipotent. Since Y and Z commute, e^Y and e^Z commute. Thus the exponential converts additive Jordan decomposition into multiplicative Jordan decomposition.

For an invertible matrix a, its multiplicative decomposition a = bc has b semisimple, c unipotent, and bc = cb. Uniqueness follows from the uniqueness of the usual Jordan decomposition: the semisimple spectral part and the nilpotent/unipotent correction are forced by the primary decomposition. Conceptually, exp respects the two different kinds of structure separately: diagonalizable behavior goes to diagonalizable behavior, and nilpotent behavior goes to unipotent behavior.

§1.2 · 7 — Small norm condition: exp(X)v = v iff Xv = 0

Question. For sufficiently small norm X, show that exp(X)v = v if and only if Xv = 0.

Solution. One direction is immediate: if Xv = 0, then every positive power X^k v is zero, so exp(X)v = v. The converse is where the small-norm hypothesis matters. If exp(X)v = v, then (exp X - I)v = 0.

For ||X|| small, h(X) is invertible because h(0)=1 and invertibility is stable under small perturbation. Thus X h(X)v = 0 implies Xv = 0 after applying h(X)^{-1}, since h(X) commutes with X as a power series in X.

Without the small-norm condition this fails: exp can wrap nonzero directions back to 1, for example through eigenvalues 2 pi i. The hypothesis keeps the logarithmic branch local and prevents periodic cancellation.
