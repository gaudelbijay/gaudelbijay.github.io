§1.1 · 5 — Exponential of a Jordan block

Question. For the $n\times n$ Jordan block $J=\lambda I+N$, show that $\exp(J)$ has $e^\lambda/k!$ on the $k$th superdiagonal.

Answer
Visual guide

Solution. Write $J=\lambda I+N$, where $N$ has $1$ on the first superdiagonal and $N^n=0$. Because $\lambda I$ commutes with $N$, the exponential splits as $\exp(\lambda I+N)=\exp(\lambda I)\exp(N)$.

$$e^J=e^{\lambda}\left(I+N+\frac{N^2}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{N^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\right).$$

The matrix $N^k$ has $1$ on the $k$th superdiagonal and $0$ elsewhere. Hence the $k$th superdiagonal of $e^J$ is $e^\lambda/k!$. The nilpotent part is what makes the infinite series become a finite polynomial.

§1.1 · 6 — Exponential of the derivative operator

Question. Let $E$ be the polynomials of degree at most $n-1$ and $D=d/d\xi$. Show that $\exp(\tau D)f(\xi)=f(\xi+\tau)$.

Answer

Solution. On this finite-dimensional space, $D^n=0$, so the exponential $e^{\tau D}$ is a finite operator polynomial.

$$e^{\tau D}f(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\tau^k}{k!}D^k f(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\tau^k}{k!}f^{(k)}(\xi).$$

The last expression is Taylor expansion of $f$ at $\xi$, evaluated at $\xi+\tau$. Thus $e^{\tau D}$ is the translation operator. This is the substitution principle in its cleanest form: the exponential recipe accepts an operator as input and produces a geometric action.

§1.2 · 1 — Nilpotent matrices and unipotent matrices

Question. Show that $X\mapsto \exp X$ maps nilpotent matrices bijectively onto unipotent matrices, with inverse $a\mapsto \log a$.

Answer
Visual guide

Solution. If $X^m=0$, then $e^X$ is $I$ plus a polynomial in $X$ with zero constant term. That extra part is nilpotent, so $e^X$ is unipotent.

$$e^X=I+X+\frac{X^2}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{X^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}.$$

Conversely, if $a=I+N$ with $N$ nilpotent, then $\log(I+N)$ is also finite:

$$\log(I+N)=N-\frac{N^2}{2}+\frac{N^3}{3}-\cdots.$$

The scalar formal identities $\exp(\log(1+z))=1+z$ and $\log(\exp z)=z$ become finite polynomial identities after substituting a nilpotent matrix. Therefore $\exp$ and $\log$ are inverse bijections between nilpotent and unipotent matrices.

§1.2 · 2 — Semisimple matrices and the exponential

Question. Show that $\exp$ maps semisimple matrices to semisimple matrices, that every invertible semisimple complex matrix is an exponential of a semisimple matrix, and explain the logarithm ambiguity.

Answer
Visual guide

Solution. If $X=PDP^{-1}$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$, then powers of $D$ stay diagonal, so the exponential is computed entry by entry:

$$e^X=P\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1},\ldots,e^{\lambda_n})P^{-1}.$$

Hence $e^X$ is semisimple. Conversely, if $a=P\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)P^{-1}$ is invertible, choose $\lambda_j$ with $e^{\lambda_j}=\mu_j$ and set $X=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)P^{-1}$.

The ambiguity is exactly $\lambda_j\mapsto \lambda_j+2\pi i k_j$. If distinct eigenvalues do not differ by a nonzero multiple of $2\pi i$, exponentiation does not merge their eigenspaces; otherwise, different logarithm branches can collapse to the same exponential.

§1.2 · 3 — Jordan decomposition and the exponential

Question. Relate additive Jordan decomposition $X=Y+Z$ to multiplicative Jordan decomposition of $\exp X$.

Answer
Visual guide

Solution. In the additive Jordan decomposition, $Y$ is semisimple, $Z$ is nilpotent, and $YZ=ZY$. Since the parts commute,

$$e^X=e^{Y+Z}=e^Y e^Z.$$

By the previous semisimple result, $e^Y$ is semisimple. By the nilpotent-unipotent result, $e^Z$ is unipotent. They commute because $Y$ and $Z$ commute. Thus the exponential turns additive Jordan decomposition into multiplicative Jordan decomposition.

For an invertible matrix $a$, the multiplicative decomposition $a=bc$ has $b$ semisimple, $c$ unipotent, and $bc=cb$. The point is structural: diagonalizable behavior and nilpotent behavior survive exponentiation as separate finite effects.

§1.2 · 6 — Cayley-Hamilton reduction of power series

Question. Use Cayley-Hamilton to show that a convergent matrix power series $f(X)$ can be written as a polynomial in $X$ of degree less than $n$.

Answer

Solution. Cayley-Hamilton says $p_X(X)=0$. If $p_X(t)=t^n+s_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+s_0$, then

$$X^n=-s_{n-1}X^{n-1}-\cdots-s_1X-s_0I.$$

Multiplying by powers of $X$ reduces every $X^k$ with $k\ge n$ to a linear combination of $I,X,\ldots,X^{n-1}$. Therefore, once the series defining $f(X)$ has converged, its value can be represented as

$$f(X)=c_0(X)I+c_1(X)X+\cdots+c_{n-1}(X)X^{n-1}.$$

This does not prove convergence. It says finite-dimensional matrix functions can be compressed after convergence is known.

§1.2 · 7 — Small norm condition: $\exp(X)v=v$ iff $Xv=0$

Question. For sufficiently small $\lVert X\rVert$, show that $\exp(X)v=v$ if and only if $Xv=0$.

Answer

Solution. If $Xv=0$, then $X^kv=0$ for all $k\ge1$, so $e^Xv=v$. Conversely, suppose $e^Xv=v$. Then $(e^X-I)v=0$.

$$e^X-I=Xh(X),\qquad h(z)=\frac{e^z-1}{z}=1+\frac{z}{2!}+\frac{z^2}{3!}+\cdots.$$

For $\lVert X\rVert$ small, $h(X)$ is invertible because $h(0)=1$ and invertibility is stable under small perturbations. Since $h(X)$ commutes with $X$, the equation $Xh(X)v=0$ implies $Xv=0$.

The smallness hypothesis prevents exponential wrapping, such as eigenvalues differing by $2\pi i$, from producing fixed vectors that are not killed infinitesimally.

§1.2 · 8 — Jacobi identity and Lie bracket derivations

Question. Prove the Jacobi identity and deduce that $\operatorname{ad}(Z)$ is a derivation of the bracket.

Answer

Solution. Expand every commutator using $[X,Y]=XY-YX$. Because matrix multiplication is associative, each triple product appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign.

$$[[X,Y],Z]+[[Z,X],Y]+[[Y,Z],X]=0.$$

Rearranging this identity with $\operatorname{ad}_Z(W)=[Z,W]$ gives

$$\operatorname{ad}_Z([X,Y])=[\operatorname{ad}_Z X,Y]+[X,\operatorname{ad}_Z Y].$$

Thus bracketing with $Z$ differentiates the bracket product. This is why $\operatorname{ad}$ is the infinitesimal form of conjugation rather than just another linear map.

§1.2 · 12 — Invariant subspaces under adjoint action

Question. If $L$ is a subspace with $[X,L]\subset L$, show that $\exp(\tau X)L\exp(-\tau X)\subset L$.

Answer

Solution. For $Y\in L$, define $F(\tau)=e^{\tau X}Ye^{-\tau X}$. Differentiating gives

$$F\prime(\tau)=XF(\tau)-F(\tau)X=[X,F(\tau)].$$

At $\tau=0$, $F(0)=Y\in L$. The assumption $[X,L]\subset L$ says this differential equation has velocity tangent to $L$ whenever $F(\tau)$ lies in $L$. Hence the solution stays in $L$.

Equivalently, $F(\tau)=e^{\tau\operatorname{ad}_X}Y$. Since $\operatorname{ad}_X$ maps $L$ into $L$, every power of $\operatorname{ad}_X$ maps $L$ into $L$, and so does its exponential.
