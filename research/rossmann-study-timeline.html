Wulf Rossmann Lie Groups: An Introduction Through Linear Groups 6 chapters · 3 phases

Every chapter of Rossmann answers one geometric question. Chapter 1 asks: what is the shape of a group near the identity? Chapter 2 asks: how curved is it? Chapters 3 and 4 give you a zoo of examples and the right setting. Chapters 5 and 6 ask the deepest question: what does it mean to do Fourier analysis on a curved space? Phase One

The Shape of a Group

Chapters 1 and 2. The group is a curved space, the Lie algebra is its flat shadow, and the commutator is the measurement of how far the shadow deviates from the truth.

Phase 1  ·  Chapter 1  ·  in progress

The Exponential Map

Begin here: a Lie group $G$ is a smooth manifold that is also a group, and the two structures are compatible. The identity element $e \in G$ has a tangent space $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ — flat, linear, and completely ordinary as a vector space. This tangent space is the Lie algebra. The central object of Chapter 1 is the map that connects them.

The exponential map $\exp: \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is defined by following the unique one-parameter subgroup through the identity in the direction $X$. Concretely, for matrix Lie groups it is the matrix exponential:

$$e^X = I + X + \frac{X^2}{2!} + \frac{X^3}{3!} + \cdots$$

The one-parameter subgroup property — $e^{(s+t)X} = e^{sX} \cdot e^{tX}$ — is the key fact. It says that moving at constant velocity $X$ for time $s$ then time $t$ is the same as moving for time $s+t$. The curve $\{e^{tX} : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ is therefore the geodesic through $e$ in the direction $X$, and the exponential map $t \mapsto e^{tX}$ parameterizes it at unit speed.

For $G = \mathrm{SE}(2)$, writing $X = \omega E_\omega + v_x E_x + v_y E_y$ with $(\omega, v_x, v_y) \in \mathbb{R}^3$, the exponential has a closed form. When $\omega \neq 0$ the key identity is $X^3 = -\omega^2 X$, which collapses the infinite series to three terms:

$$e^X = I + \frac{\sin\omega}{\omega}\,X + \frac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega^2}\,X^2 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\omega & -\sin\omega & \frac{\sin\omega}{\omega}v_x - \frac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega}v_y \\ \sin\omega & \cos\omega & \frac{1-\cos\omega}{\omega}v_x + \frac{\sin\omega}{\omega}v_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Look at this carefully. A rotation by angle $\omega$ and a translation — but the translation is not simply $(v_x, v_y)^T$. It is $(v_x, v_y)^T$ mixed with $\omega$ via the Jacobian $J(\omega)$. The group is already telling you something: rotation and translation do not act independently, they couple through the Jacobian. This coupling is the geometric seed of everything in Chapter 2.

A crucial theorem: $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism near $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$. This means you can use the Lie algebra as a coordinate chart for the group near the identity. Every group element sufficiently close to $e$ is $e^X$ for a unique small $X$. The Lie algebra is the group's own coordinate system — the natural language in which to measure infinitesimal displacements.

What you see after Chapter 1

The group is a curved space, and you have a coordinate system for it near the identity: the Lie algebra. Every velocity $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ defines a geodesic $e^{tX}$, and the exponential map is the "integration" of that velocity over unit time. The closed form for $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ already hints that the geometry is non-trivial: the coupling between rotation and translation in the translation part of $e^X$ is not accidental — it is the first shadow of the curvature you will measure in Chapter 2.

The question Chapter 1 leaves open

You now know how to move on the group: choose a velocity $X$, follow $e^{tX}$, arrive at $e^X$. But what happens when you choose two velocities and move in each direction in sequence? Is $e^X \cdot e^Y$ the same as $e^{X+Y}$? On a flat space — like $\mathbb{R}^n$ under addition — yes, trivially. On a curved group, no. And the amount by which the answer deviates from $e^{X+Y}$ is the entire subject of Chapter 2.

Phase 1  ·  Chapter 2  ·  the hardest chapter

Lie Theory

Here is the central question: given $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$, what is $\log(e^X e^Y)$? On the vector space $\mathbb{R}^n$ the answer is trivial — $X + Y$ — because addition commutes. On a curved group, the product $e^X e^Y$ lands somewhere that is not $e^{X+Y}$, and computing exactly where it lands requires understanding the curvature of $G$.

The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff theorem provides the answer as a series in nested commutators:

$$\log(e^X e^Y) = X + Y + \frac{1}{2}[X,Y] + \frac{1}{12}\bigl([X,[X,Y]] + [Y,[Y,X]]\bigr) + \frac{1}{24}[Y,[X,[X,Y]]] + \cdots$$

Read this geometrically, term by term. The first two terms, $X + Y$, are what you get on a flat space. The correction $\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]$ is the first-order curvature: the infinitesimal holonomy of the loop that goes along $X$, then $Y$, then back along $X$, then back along $Y$. The double commutators $[X,[X,Y]]$ are the second-order curvature — the twist you accumulate by rotating twice. Every term in BCH is a geometric quantity: it measures a deeper and deeper layer of how the group bends.

For $\mathfrak{se}(2)$, the commutator $[X_1, X_2]$ in coordinates $(\omega, v_x, v_y)$ is:

$$[X_1, X_2] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -(\omega_1 v_{y,2} - \omega_2 v_{y,1}) \\ \omega_1 v_{x,2} - \omega_2 v_{x,1} \end{pmatrix}$$

Notice: the rotation component is always zero. Pure rotations commute with each other ($[E_\omega, E_\omega] = 0$, trivially), so the curvature of $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ lives entirely in the translation components. The commutator is non-zero only when one motion has a rotational part and the other has a translational part — the rotation acts on the translation and produces a correction. This is precisely the coupling you already saw in the off-diagonal terms of $e^X$.

The adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad}: G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ is defined by $\mathrm{Ad}(g) X = g X g^{-1}$ — the group acting on its own Lie algebra by conjugation. Its derivative at the identity is the adjoint map $\mathrm{ad}: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathrm{End}(\mathfrak{g})$, defined by $\mathrm{ad}(X)(Y) = [X,Y]$. These two — $\mathrm{Ad}$ and $\mathrm{ad}$ — are connected by the beautiful formula:

$$\mathrm{Ad}(e^X) = e^{\mathrm{ad}(X)} = I + [X, \cdot] + \frac{1}{2}[X,[X,\cdot]] + \cdots$$

This formula is not just elegant — it is the fundamental equivariance law of the group acting on itself. Conjugation distributes over the Lie bracket: $[\mathrm{Ad}(h)X, \mathrm{Ad}(h)Y] = \mathrm{Ad}(h)[X,Y]$. This means that the BCH series transforms correctly under conjugation at every order — the group's curvature is itself equivariant under the group's own action. This is why the adjoint representation is so central: it is the group looking at its own geometry.

One more fact from Chapter 2 that is worth pausing on: the Jacobi identity, $[X,[Y,Z]] + [Y,[Z,X]] + [Z,[X,Y]] = 0$. Algebraically it looks like an arbitrary axiom. Geometrically it is the statement that the curvature operator satisfies the Bianchi identity — the same identity that appears in general relativity for the Riemann curvature tensor. The Lie algebra is a flat space whose "curvature" (the commutator) satisfies a Bianchi identity. This is not a coincidence; it is the deepest reason why Lie groups are tractable.

What you see after Chapter 2

The Lie bracket is not an algebraic definition — it is the infinitesimal holonomy. BCH is not a formula — it is the complete measurement of the group's curvature, expressed in coordinates. A group with $[X,Y] = 0$ for all $X, Y$ is abelian — it is flat, and BCH collapses to $X + Y$ exactly. $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ is curved, and the BCH correction terms capture exactly how a translation gets rotated when you compose it with a rotation — the geometry of the group is inseparable from its algebra.

Phase 1 Complete — The core geometry is in hand

After Chapter 2 you possess the essential vocabulary of Lie theory: the exponential map as the bridge between algebra and geometry, the Lie bracket as curvature, BCH as the composition law on the group written in algebraic coordinates, and $\mathrm{Ad}$ as the group reflecting on its own tangent space. Chapters 3–6 broaden the picture with examples, put it on rigorous foundations, and culminate in the beautiful theorem that ties everything together.

Phase Two

The Menagerie

Chapters 3 and 4. You have the abstract theory; now you need examples. Chapter 3 gives you a zoo of groups you can visualize. Chapter 4 gives you the right setting for everything you've done so far.

Phase 2  ·  Chapter 3

The Classical Groups and Their Lie Algebras

After two chapters of abstract theory you need to feel the geometry in specific examples. Chapter 3 provides them: the orthogonal groups $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, the unitary groups $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, the symplectic groups $\mathrm{Sp}(n)$, and the Euclidean groups $\mathrm{SE}(n)$.

$\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is literally a circle. Every rotation in the plane is a point on the unit circle, the group multiplication is addition of angles, the Lie algebra is $\mathbb{R}$ with zero bracket ($[a, b] = 0$ for all $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$), and the exponential map is $\omega \mapsto e^{i\omega}$ — the familiar winding of the real line onto the circle. This is as flat as a group can be. BCH collapses to $\omega_1 + \omega_2$ exactly.

$\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is something richer: a 3-dimensional curved space. Every rotation in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is uniquely determined by its axis (a unit vector $\hat{n} \in S^2$) and angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$, so $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is topologically a 3-ball of radius $\pi$ with antipodal boundary points identified — the projective space $\mathbb{RP}^3$. Its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ has the bracket $[X, Y] = X \times Y$ (the cross product). The BCH formula for $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the Rodrigues formula for composing rotations — and it is visibly non-commutative. Everyone who has tried to rotate a 3D object knows this: rotating 90° around $x$ then 90° around $y$ gives a different result than $y$ first then $x$. The gap between these two results is the commutator $X \times Y$.

$\mathrm{SU}(2)$, the group of $2 \times 2$ unitary matrices with determinant 1, has the same Lie algebra as $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ — same bracket, same commutator structure — but it is topologically $S^3$, the 3-sphere. The map $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is a $2$-to-$1$ covering: every rotation in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ comes from exactly two elements of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, differing by $\pm 1$. This is the mathematical explanation for the spinor double cover of physical rotations.

$\mathrm{SE}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SE}(3)$ appear as semidirect products: $\mathrm{SE}(n) = \mathrm{SO}(n) \ltimes \mathbb{R}^n$. The symbol $\ltimes$ encodes the action: $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ acts on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by rotation, so the two parts do not commute. This action is precisely the adjoint action from Chapter 2: $\mathrm{Ad}(g(\theta, \mathbf{t}))$ rotates a translation vector by the rotation part of $g$. The non-zero commutators in $\mathfrak{se}(2)$ that you computed in Chapter 2 are exactly the infinitesimal expression of this action.

What you see after Chapter 3

The abstract Lie bracket has a concrete feel: it is the cross product of rotation axes in $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, it is the coupling of rotation and translation in $\mathrm{SE}(2)$. Two groups can share the same Lie algebra (the same local geometry) but have different global topology — $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ are the canonical example. This distinction matters when you choose which representations to use in Chapter 6: the topology of $G$ constrains which irreps exist.

The question Chapter 3 leaves open

You have seen that two groups — $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ — share the same Lie algebra but are topologically different. Their local geometry is identical; their global structure is not. The Lie algebra only sees the group near the identity. Chapter 4 makes this precise: it asks what the Lie algebra can and cannot tell you about the group, and introduces the language of manifolds that makes the distinction between local and global rigorous.

Phase 2  ·  Chapter 4  ·  most skippable

Manifolds, Homomorphisms, Lie Groups

Until now, "Lie group" meant "closed subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$" — a matrix group. Chapter 4 gives the general definition: a Lie group is a smooth manifold $G$ equipped with smooth group operations $(g, h) \mapsto gh$ and $g \mapsto g^{-1}$. Matrix groups are a special case. The manifold formalism is needed to talk rigorously about the tangent bundle, smooth maps between groups, and global topology.

The central theorem of this chapter — and one of the central theorems in all of Lie theory — is the Lie correspondence: there is a bijection between simply connected Lie groups and finite-dimensional Lie algebras (over $\mathbb{R}$). Given a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, there is a unique simply connected Lie group $\tilde{G}$ with $T_e \tilde{G} = \mathfrak{g}$, called the universal cover. Every other connected Lie group with the same Lie algebra is a quotient $\tilde{G}/\Gamma$ for some discrete central subgroup $\Gamma \subset Z(\tilde{G})$.

For $\mathfrak{so}(3)$: the universal cover is $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3$, and $\mathrm{SO}(3) = \mathrm{SU}(2)/\{\pm I\}$ is the quotient by the two-element central subgroup. For $\mathfrak{se}(2)$: the universal cover is $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ itself — it is already simply connected (its fundamental group is $\pi_1(\mathrm{SE}(2)) = \pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, wait, actually $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ is not simply connected — it has the homotopy type of $S^1$ — so its universal cover is $\mathbb{R} \ltimes \mathbb{R}^2$, covering it by unwinding the rotation angle).

The practical consequence for Chapter 6: the representations of a group $G$ are exactly the representations of its universal cover $\tilde{G}$ that factor through the quotient map. For $\mathrm{SE}(2)$, this means the irreps must be periodic in the rotation angle with period $2\pi$ — they must be well-defined on the circle, not just on the real line. This is why the irrep indices are integers $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ (for the rotational part) rather than real numbers.

What you see after Chapter 4

The Lie algebra is a local invariant — it sees only the group near the identity. The global topology of $G$ (its fundamental group, its covering spaces) determines which representations exist and which Fourier frequencies are allowed. For $\mathrm{SE}(2)$, the integer-valued rotation indices $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ come directly from the fact that $\mathrm{SO}(2) \cong S^1$ has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}$. Skim this chapter for the main ideas; return to the proofs if you need them later.

Phase Three

Fourier Analysis on Curved Space

Chapters 5 and 6. The group acquires a canonical measure, functions on it form a Hilbert space, and the Peter–Weyl theorem gives that Hilbert space its natural orthonormal basis — one that is simultaneously a generalization of the Fourier series and the representation theory of the group.

Why Fourier analysis on a group is the right question

The classical Fourier transform on $\mathbb{R}$ decomposes a signal into frequencies $e^{i\xi x}$ — functions that are eigenvectors of the translation operator. On a Lie group $G$, the natural objects to decompose into are the matrix coefficients of irreducible representations — functions that transform in the simplest possible way under group multiplication. The question "which basis is natural for $L^2(G)$?" is exactly the question of finding the group's irreducible representations. This is Peter–Weyl's answer: the irrep matrix coefficients are the Fourier modes of the group.

Phase 3  ·  Chapter 5

Integration on Lie Groups

To do analysis on a group — to define $L^2(G)$, to compute inner products, to state orthogonality theorems — you need a measure. The question is: which measure? On $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Lebesgue measure is distinguished by its translation invariance: $\int f(x + a)\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx$ for all $a$. On a Lie group, the analogous condition is left-invariance: a measure $\mu$ is left-invariant if $\mu(gE) = \mu(E)$ for all $g \in G$ and measurable sets $E$. The Haar theorem states that such a measure exists and is unique up to scaling. This is the Haar measure.

For compact groups (like $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(n)$), the Haar measure is also right-invariant — the group is unimodular. For non-compact groups, left and right invariance can differ; the ratio is measured by the modular function $\Delta: G \to \mathbb{R}^+$, which is a continuous group homomorphism. $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ is unimodular, and its Haar measure has a beautifully simple form: in coordinates $(\theta, t_x, t_y)$ for $\mathrm{SE}(2)$ (angle plus translation), the Haar measure is simply $d\theta\, dt_x\, dt_y$ — just the flat Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^3$. The group's curved multiplication law is invisible in the measure: the geometry is in the group law, not in the volume element.

With the Haar measure, you define the Hilbert space $L^2(G) = \{ f: G \to \mathbb{C} : \int_G |f(g)|^2\, d\mu(g) < \infty \}$ with inner product:

$$\langle f, h \rangle = \int_G f(g)\,\overline{h(g)}\,d\mu(g)$$

This inner product is left-invariant: $\langle L_k f, L_k h \rangle = \langle f, h \rangle$ where $(L_k f)(g) = f(k^{-1}g)$. This invariance is the key. It means that the decomposition of $L^2(G)$ into irreducible subspaces is compatible with the group action — which is exactly the Peter–Weyl theorem.

What you see after Chapter 5

The group is not just a space you move through — it has a canonical volume. Functions on it form a Hilbert space with a natural inner product. The measure is invariant under the group's own action on itself, which means any decomposition of $L^2(G)$ that respects the inner product will automatically be compatible with the group structure. This is the setup for Peter–Weyl, which says: the "best" decomposition — the one into irreducible pieces — is also the natural Fourier decomposition.

Phase 3  ·  Chapter 6  ·  the payoff

Representations and the Peter–Weyl Theorem

A unitary representation of $G$ is a continuous group homomorphism $\pi: G \to U(V)$ from $G$ into the group of unitary operators on a Hilbert space $V$ — a way of making $G$ act linearly and isometrically on a vector space. An irreducible representation (irrep) is one that has no proper closed invariant subspace: it is atomic, cannot be further decomposed. Schur's lemma characterizes them: the only operators that commute with every $\pi(g)$ are scalar multiples of the identity. This is what "irreducible" truly means — the representation has no internal symmetry beyond the group itself.

From an irrep $\pi: G \to U(V_\pi)$ with basis $\{e_m\}$ of $V_\pi$, you extract the matrix coefficient functions:

$$\pi_{mn}: G \to \mathbb{C}, \qquad \pi_{mn}(g) = \langle e_m, \pi(g) e_n \rangle$$

These are ordinary functions on the group — one for each pair of basis indices $(m, n)$ and each irrep $\pi$. The Peter–Weyl theorem says they form an orthogonal basis for $L^2(G)$:

$$\langle \pi_{mn}, \rho_{kl} \rangle_{L^2(G)} = \frac{1}{\dim V_\pi}\,\delta_{\pi\rho}\,\delta_{mk}\,\delta_{nl}$$

This orthogonality is the group-theoretic analogue of the Fourier orthogonality $\langle e^{imx}, e^{inx} \rangle = \delta_{mn}$. For $G = S^1 = \mathrm{SO}(2)$, the irreps are exactly the one-dimensional representations $\pi_n(e^{i\theta}) = e^{in\theta}$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the matrix coefficients are $\pi_n(\theta) = e^{in\theta}$, and Peter–Weyl is the classical Fourier series theorem. The Peter–Weyl theorem is therefore the generalization of Fourier analysis from the circle to an arbitrary Lie group.

The completeness part of Peter–Weyl says that every $f \in L^2(G)$ can be written as:

$$f(g) = \sum_{\pi \in \hat{G}} \dim(V_\pi) \sum_{m,n} \hat{f}_{mn}^\pi \,\pi_{mn}(g)$$

where $\hat{f}_{mn}^\pi = \langle f, \pi_{mn} \rangle_{L^2(G)}$ are the generalized Fourier coefficients and $\hat{G}$ is the set of equivalence classes of irreps. The "spectrum" of the group — the set $\hat{G}$ of all irreps — completely characterizes the group's $L^2$ analysis, just as the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ characterize the Fourier analysis of $S^1$.

The Peter–Weyl coefficients $\hat{f}_{mn}^\pi$ are the natural coordinates on $L^2(G)$. Any linear map $T: L^2(G) \to L^2(G)$ that commutes with all left translations — that is, $T \circ L_g = L_g \circ T$ for all $g \in G$ — must, by Schur's lemma, act as a scalar on each irrep subspace. This means equivariant linear maps are exactly the block-diagonal maps in the Peter–Weyl basis. Equivariance is not an additional constraint to impose — it is built into the choice of basis.

What you see after Chapter 6

Functions on a group have a canonical frequency decomposition, and the "frequencies" are the irreducible representations. On $\mathbb{R}$, the frequencies are $e^{i\xi x}$ — eigenfunctions of the translation operator. On $G$, the frequencies are the matrix coefficients of irreps — eigenfunctions of the left-translation operators $L_g$. Fourier analysis is not a special trick for the real line; it is a universal phenomenon that emerges whenever you have a group and a Haar measure.

The Peter–Weyl decomposition is the deepest answer to the question that opened Chapter 1: what is the natural structure of a Lie group? The answer is that every function on the group decomposes canonically into irreducible pieces, each transforming in a single, atomic way under the group action. This decomposition is as natural and inevitable as the decomposition of a periodic function into Fourier modes — and for exactly the same reason.

Phase 3 Complete — The theory is whole

You now hold the complete picture. The group is a curved space (Ch 1). The curvature is measured by the Lie bracket and encoded by BCH (Ch 2). The classical groups give that picture flesh and examples (Ch 3). The manifold structure clarifies what the Lie algebra can and cannot see (Ch 4). The Haar measure makes analysis possible (Ch 5). And Peter–Weyl says the natural basis for that analysis is exactly the set of irreducible representations (Ch 6). Each chapter was not merely the next topic — it was the inevitable next question.

Beyond Rossmann

The Non-Compact Case: SE(2)

Rossmann focuses on compact groups, where every irrep is finite-dimensional. SE(2) is non-compact — its irreps are infinite-dimensional, labeled by a continuous momentum parameter, and their matrix coefficients are Bessel functions.

Beyond Rossmann  ·  Sugiura Ch 2

Unitary Representations and Harmonic Analysis

Rossmann's Peter–Weyl theorem applies to compact Lie groups, where the theory is clean: all irreps are finite-dimensional, the sum over $\hat{G}$ is discrete, and the matrix coefficients are smooth bounded functions. For non-compact groups like $\mathrm{SE}(2)$, the story is more delicate, and requires Mackey's theory of induced representations.

$\mathrm{SE}(2) = \mathrm{SO}(2) \ltimes \mathbb{R}^2$ is a semidirect product. Mackey's machine says: to classify irreps of a semidirect product $N \rtimes H$, look at the orbits of $H$ acting on $\hat{N}$ (the Pontryagin dual of $N$). For $\mathrm{SE}(2)$: $N = \mathbb{R}^2$ with $\hat{N} = \mathbb{R}^2$ (by Fourier duality), and $H = \mathrm{SO}(2)$ acts on $\hat{\mathbb{R}}^2$ by rotation. The orbits of $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ are circles of radius $p \geq 0$. Each orbit gives one irrep family:

$$\pi^p_{mn}(g(\theta, \mathbf{t})) = e^{-in\theta} \cdot J_{m-n}(p|\mathbf{t}|) \cdot e^{i(m-n)\arg(\mathbf{t})}$$

where $J_k$ is the Bessel function of the first kind of order $k$, and $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$. The index $p \geq 0$ is the momentum — the radius of the SO(2)-orbit in Fourier space. The index $n$ labels the angular frequency within each momentum shell. When $\mathbf{t} = 0$ (pure rotation), the matrix coefficients reduce to $e^{-in\theta} \delta_{mn}$, recovering the SO(2) Fourier modes.

Bessel functions are not mysterious in this context — they are the natural harmonics of the plane. The function $J_k(p r) e^{ik\phi}$ (in polar coordinates $(r, \phi)$) is a simultaneous eigenfunction of the 2D Laplacian with eigenvalue $-p^2$ and of the rotation operator with eigenvalue $e^{ik\phi}$. These are the "plane waves" of $\mathbb{R}^2$ with circular symmetry, and they appear as SE(2) irrep matrix elements for exactly this reason: the irreps of SE(2) are the natural "frequency modes" of functions on the plane that transform correctly under both rotation and translation.

Why Bessel functions appear — the geometric reason

SE(2) acts on the plane. The "eigenfunctions" of this action — the functions that transform in the simplest possible way under both rotation and translation simultaneously — are the Bessel functions. The rotation index $n$ tracks how the function spins as you rotate the plane; the momentum $p$ tracks its spatial frequency. Translation couples rotation and spatial frequency through the Bessel function, because translating a spinning function mixes its angular modes. This coupling is the non-compact analogue of the coupling between rotation and translation that you first saw in the off-diagonal terms of $e^X$ in Chapter 1.

The single thread

Every chapter is an answer to the same question, asked at a deeper level: what is the natural structure of a space with symmetry? The exponential map is the natural coordinate system. The Lie bracket is the natural measure of curvature. The classical groups are the natural examples. The Haar measure is the natural volume.
